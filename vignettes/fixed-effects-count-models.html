Fixed-effects count models

Panel count data, such as crashes on the same road segments over several years, contain differences between units that the regressors do not capture: driver populations, geometry, enforcement. Random-effects models (renb(), repois(), countreg_rp() with a random intercept) treat these differences as draws from a distribution that is independent of the regressors. When that is doubtful (busier roads may also be different roads) a fixed-effects model gives each unit its own intercept, so the slopes are estimated only from changes within units.

countreg_fe() estimates fixed-effects count models. This article shows when to use it, what it can and cannot estimate, and how its results compare with pooled and random-effects models.

library(flexCountReg)
data("washington_roads")

1. The model

For unit \(i\) (here a road segment) in period \(t\),

\[ E[y_{it} \mid x_{it}, \alpha_i] = \exp(\alpha_i + x_{it}'\beta + o_{it}), \]

with a fixed effect \(\alpha_i\) for each unit and an optional offset \(o_{it}\). Nothing is assumed about how \(\alpha_i\) relates to \(x_{it}\).

countreg_fe() chooses the estimator from the family and the number of fixed-effect dimensions:

Family Fixed effects Method Notes
Poisson one (~ site) conditional likelihood the effects are conditioned out; consistent for fixed T with only a correct mean (Wooldridge 1999)
Poisson several (~ site + year) IRLS with alternating projections the method of fixest::fepois()
any other one concentrated likelihood each unit’s intercept is solved for given slopes; small-T bias in the dispersion

Poisson fixed effects are the usual first choice: the slopes are consistent even when the counts are overdispersed or not Poisson at all, as long as the mean is right, and the default cluster-robust standard errors account for the overdispersion.

2. Site fixed effects

Here washington_roads has 507 segments, most observed in three years. Traffic volume (lnaadt) changes from year to year within a segment, but the speed indicator speed50 does not:

fe <- countreg_fe(Total_crashes ~ lnaadt + speed50 + offset(lnlength),
                  fixef = ~ ID, data = washington_roads)
#> 804 row(s) in 273 group(s) with all-zero counts or a single observation were dropped: they carry no information on the slopes.
#> Warning: Regressor(s) without variation within the fixed-effect groups are not
#> identified and were removed: speed50.
summary(fe)

Two things happened before estimation, and both are reported:

  • Segments with no crashes in any year were dropped. Their fixed effect would be \(-\infty\); they carry no information on the slopes. So were segments observed only once.
  • speed50 was removed. A regressor that is constant within every unit is absorbed by the unit’s fixed effect, so its coefficient is not identified. Time-invariant characteristics can only be studied with random-effects or pooled models.
length(fe$model$dropped$ID)     # segments dropped
#> [1] 273
fe$model$not_identified         # regressors removed
#> [1] "speed50"

The standard errors are cluster-robust by segment (stderr = "cluster", the default). Other covariance matrices are available after the fit:

sqrt(diag(vcov(fe)))                      # clustered by segment
#>    lnaadt 
#> 0.4912069
sqrt(diag(vcov(fe, type = "hessian")))    # Poisson model-based
#>    lnaadt 
#> 0.6052379

The model-based standard error is much smaller: it assumes the Poisson variance, which these counts exceed.

3. Site and year fixed effects

Crash counts often share year-to-year changes (weather, reporting, the economy). A second fixed-effect dimension absorbs them:

fe2 <- countreg_fe(Total_crashes ~ lnaadt + offset(lnlength),
                   fixef = ~ ID + Year, data = washington_roads)
#> 804 row(s) in 273 group(s) with all-zero counts or a single observation were dropped: they carry no information on the slopes.

With few levels, a dimension like Year can also enter the formula as a factor; with several high-dimensional effects (sites and corridor-years, say), the alternating projections keep the computation fast.

4. Comparison with pooled and random-effects models

The elasticity of crashes with respect to traffic volume from the pooled, random-effects and fixed-effects Poisson models:

f <- Total_crashes ~ lnaadt + offset(lnlength)
pooled <- countreg(f, data = washington_roads, family = "POISSON",
                   method = "BFGS", cluster = ~ ID)
re <- repois(f, data = washington_roads, group_var = "ID")
est <- function(m) c(estimate = coef(m)[["lnaadt"]],
                     se = sqrt(diag(vcov(m)))[["lnaadt"]])
round(rbind(pooled = est(pooled), random_effects = est(re),
            fixed_effects = est(fe2)), 3)
#>                estimate    se
#> pooled            1.196 0.078
#> random_effects    1.144 0.065
#> fixed_effects    -0.576 0.483

The pooled and random-effects estimates use the differences between segments in traffic volume, which are large; the fixed-effects estimate uses only the changes within segments over three years, which are small, so its standard error is much larger. A big gap between the random-effects and fixed-effects estimates suggests that the segment effects are related to traffic volume, which would bias the random-effects estimate.

5. Other families

Any family of countreg() (and any family made with new_count_family()) can be used with one fixed-effect dimension. The negative binomial (NB2) fixed-effects model is the dummy-variable NB2, with the unit intercepts concentrated out.

For the road segments, NB2 fixed effects reduce to the Poisson model: once each segment has its own intercept, no overdispersion remains in three years of counts, and the estimate of \(\alpha\) goes to 0 (the fit warns that the likelihood is flat in ln(alpha)). The Poisson fixed-effects model of Section 2 is then the right one.

To show the NB2 model where it matters, simulate 300 units observed for 5 periods, with unit effects correlated with x1 and overdispersed counts (NB2 with \(\alpha = 0.5\)):

set.seed(11)
G <- 300; n_t <- 5
sim <- data.frame(id = rep(seq_len(G), each = n_t),
                  t = rep(seq_len(n_t), G))
a_i <- rnorm(G, 0, 0.7)
sim$x1 <- rnorm(nrow(sim)) + 0.5 * a_i[sim$id]
sim$x2 <- rnorm(nrow(sim))
sim$y <- rnbinom(nrow(sim), size = 2,
                 mu = exp(0.5 + a_i[sim$id] + 0.6 * sim$x1 - 0.4 * sim$x2))

fe_nb <- countreg_fe(y ~ x1 + x2, fixef = ~ id, data = sim, family = "NB2")
#> 55 row(s) in 11 group(s) with all-zero counts or a single observation were dropped: they carry no information on the slopes.
fe_nb_jk <- countreg_fe(y ~ x1 + x2, fixef = ~ id, data = sim,
                        family = "NB2", jackknife = TRUE, time = "t")
#> 55 row(s) in 11 group(s) with all-zero counts or a single observation were dropped: they carry no information on the slopes.
round(rbind(truth = c(0.6, -0.4, log(0.5)),
            NB2_FE = coef(fe_nb),
            jackknife = coef(fe_nb_jk)), 3)
#>              x1     x2 ln(alpha)
#> truth     0.600 -0.400    -0.693
#> NB2_FE    0.555 -0.416    -1.238
#> jackknife 0.550 -0.420    -0.707

The slopes are close to the truth, but the dispersion \(\ln\alpha\) is underestimated, because the fixed effects absorb much of the variation between units. Two cautions apply to non-Poisson fixed effects in short panels:

  • The slopes have a small bias of order \(1/T\) (Allison and Waterman 2002); the dispersion has a large one.
  • jackknife = TRUE applies the split-panel jackknife of Dhaene and Jochmans (2015). In simulations like this one, with 200 units, it removed most of the dispersion bias with 5 or 10 periods but did not improve the slopes, and with 3 periods its half-panels are too short (a warning is given below 4 periods).

The Hausman, Hall and Griliches “conditional negative binomial” is a different model, whose fixed effect enters the dispersion, not the mean; it is not provided.

6. Correlated random effects: a test of random against fixed effects

Random-effects models can be kept, and the question of section 4 tested, with the device of Mundlak (1978): add each unit’s mean of the time-varying regressors to the model. The random effect may then depend on the regressors through their means, and the coefficient of the regressor itself is a within-unit effect, as in the fixed-effects model. cre = does this in repois(), renb() and countreg_rp():

cre <- repois(Total_crashes ~ lnaadt + offset(lnlength), group_var = "ID",
              data = washington_roads, cre = ~ lnaadt, method = "BFGS",
              stderr = "robust")
round(coef(cre)[c("lnaadt", "mean_lnaadt")], 3)
#>      lnaadt mean_lnaadt 
#>      -0.706       1.861
cre_test(cre)
#> 
#>  Wald test of correlated random effects (group means)
#> 
#> data:  repois(formula = Total_crashes ~ lnaadt + offset(lnlength), group_var = "ID",      data = washington_roads, method = "BFGS", stderr = "robust",      cre = ~lnaadt)
#> Wald chi-squared = 8.349, df = 1, p-value = 0.003859
#> sample estimates:
#> mean_lnaadt 
#>    1.860804

cre_test() is a Wald test that the coefficients of the means are zero. With robust standard errors it is a robust version of the Hausman test (Wooldridge 2019). A small p-value says that the segment effects are related to traffic volume: the plain random-effects estimate of section 4 is then biased, and the within-segment effect (the coefficient of lnaadt here, or the fixed-effects estimate) is the one to report.

In simulations with 300 units and 5 periods, when the unit effects were correlated with the regressor the plain random-effects slope was biased (0.63 for a true 0.5), while the model with cre gave 0.505, equal to the fixed-effects estimate, and cre_test() rejected in every replication. With uncorrelated effects all three were unbiased and the test rejected in 7% of replications at the 5% level.

The group means are computed on the estimation rows and stored with the fit, so predictions for a known unit use its training means: rows of newdata never change them.

7. Predictions

Predictions for a known segment use its estimated fixed effect. A segment that was not in the estimation data has no fixed effect, so its prediction is NA unless you ask for the mean effect explicitly:

nd <- washington_roads[washington_roads$ID %in%
                         names(fe2$model$fixef$fe1)[1:2], ]
predict(fe2, newdata = nd)
#> [1] 0.3414432 0.3402894 0.3244072 0.3233158 0.3341496 0.3363948

new_site <- nd[1, ]
new_site$ID <- "not-in-data"
predict(fe2, newdata = new_site)
#> 1 row(s) belong to groups not in the estimation data, which have no fixed effect: their predictions are NA (use newgroup = "mean_fe" to use the mean effect).
#> [1] NA
predict(fe2, newdata = new_site, newgroup = "mean_fe")
#> [1] 0.5565084

8. Agreement with other software

The Poisson estimates, log-likelihoods and dropped groups equal those of fixest::fepois(), and the NB2 estimates those of fixest::fenegbin() and of MASS::glm.nb() with segment dummies. The cluster-robust standard errors use the factor \(G/(G-1)\) for \(G\) clusters; they equal fixest’s with ssc(adj = FALSE), as fixest by default also multiplies the variance by \((n-1)/(n-K)\).
