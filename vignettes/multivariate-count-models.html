Multivariate count models

Crash data often hold several counts for each site: crashes of each severity, or of each type. countreg_mv() fits one count regression for each outcome in one model. Each outcome has its own family, mean formula and distribution-parameter formulas.

This is the direct way to model counts by category. A total and its split across categories is the other way; see vignette("total-allocation-models").

library(flexCountReg)
data("washington_roads")
w <- washington_roads
w$pdo <- w$Total_crashes - w$Injury_crashes
w$injury <- w$Injury_crashes
colSums(w[, c("pdo", "injury")])
#>    pdo injury 
#>    638     57

1. One family for each outcome

Outcomes can behave differently. Property damage only (PDO) crashes are frequent; injury crashes are rarer and can be more skewed. So each outcome can have its own family:

m <- countreg_mv(cbind(pdo, injury) ~ lnaadt + speed50 + offset(lnlength),
                 data = w, family = c(pdo = "NB2", injury = "PIG"))
summary(m)

The coefficients are named <outcome>:<term>. family takes one code for every outcome, or one per outcome. Different right-hand sides are given as a list of formulas, one per outcome:

m2 <- countreg_mv(list(pdo = pdo ~ lnaadt + speed50 + offset(lnlength),
                       injury = injury ~ lnaadt + offset(lnlength)),
                  data = w, family = c(pdo = "NB2", injury = "PIG"))
AIC(m, m2)
#>    df      AIC
#> m   8 2510.109
#> m2  7 2519.182

dis_param_formula_1 and dis_param_formula_2 take one formula for every outcome or a list named by outcome, so the dispersion of one outcome can depend on covariates while the others stay constant.

2. Choosing the families

The equations are fitted together, but in this version they are independent given the covariates: the log-likelihood is the sum of the equations’ log-likelihoods, and each equation’s estimates equal those of a separate countreg() fit. So the family of each outcome can be chosen by comparing fits that change only that outcome’s family:

fams <- list(c("NB2", "NB2"), c("NB2", "PIG"), c("NB2", "PLN"),
             c("PIG", "PIG"))
sapply(fams, function(f) {
  fit <- countreg_mv(cbind(pdo, injury) ~ lnaadt + speed50 +
                       offset(lnlength), data = w, family = f)
  c(pdo = f[1], injury = f[2], AIC = round(AIC(fit), 1))
})
#>        [,1]     [,2]     [,3]   [,4]    
#> pdo    "NB2"    "NB2"    "NB2"  "PIG"   
#> injury "NB2"    "PIG"    "PLN"  "PIG"   
#> AIC    "2510.2" "2510.1" "2510" "2509.9"

Here the choices fit almost equally well (the AICs are within 0.3), so the simpler NB2 is a reasonable choice for both outcomes. With rarer or more skewed outcomes the differences between families are larger.

3. Tests across equations

One fit gives the covariance of all the estimates, so hypotheses that involve several equations can be tested. Is the effect of traffic the same for PDO and injury crashes?

b <- coef(m)
V <- vcov(m)
a <- c("pdo:lnaadt", "injury:lnaadt")
diff <- b[a[1]] - b[a[2]]
se <- sqrt(V[a[1], a[1]] + V[a[2], a[2]] - 2 * V[a[1], a[2]])
c(difference = unname(diff), z = unname(diff / se),
  p = 2 * pnorm(-abs(unname(diff / se))))
#>   difference            z            p 
#> 0.5083084223 3.3436944056 0.0008267075

Traffic increases PDO crashes clearly more than injury crashes (p < 0.001), so the share of injury crashes falls as traffic grows. The covariance between the two estimates is zero here, because the equations are independent; with correlated equations it would not be.

4. Predictions

predict() gives a matrix of expected counts, one column per outcome. type = "prob" gives, for each outcome, the probabilities of the counts 0, 1, 2, …, and simulate() draws all outcomes at once:

nd <- w[c(1, 50, 200), ]
predict(m, newdata = nd)
#>             pdo     injury
#> [1,] 0.77454644 0.03005740
#> [2,] 0.09928381 0.01309018

lapply(predict(m, newdata = nd, type = "prob", max_count = 3), round, 3)

tidy() has an outcome column, and residuals(), fitted(), confint() and glance() work as for other models.

rootogram() compares the observed counts of each outcome with its own fitted distribution, one plot per outcome (outcome selects some of them). It checks the marginal distributions, not the dependence between the outcomes:

rootogram(m)

5. Correlated outcomes

The counts of different severity levels at a site are usually correlated: unobserved conditions that raise PDO crashes also raise injury crashes. rpar_formula adds random parameters to the equations, drawn once per unit of group_var (here a road segment, shared by its three years) and correlated across equations (correlation = "across", the default).

Poisson outcomes with correlated normal random intercepts give the multivariate Poisson-lognormal model (MVPLN): each segment’s intercepts in the two equations are bivariate normal, which makes each outcome overdispersed and the two outcomes correlated.

mr <- countreg_mv(cbind(pdo, injury) ~ lnaadt + speed50 + offset(lnlength),
                  data = w, family = "POISSON", rpar_formula = ~ 1,
                  group_var = "ID", rpar_draws = 200, seed = 1)
summary(mr)

The estimated correlation of the random intercepts is about 0.64: segments with more PDO crashes than their traffic and speed limit predict tend to have more injury crashes too. The evidence for it is modest (the chol: element that carries it has p of about 0.06), because injury crashes are rare. The MVPLN still fits much better than the independent NB2 and PIG equations (AIC 2472 against 2510). Other families can be used in the same way: they keep their own overdispersion within each outcome, and the random intercepts add the correlation between outcomes. Random slopes are more terms in rpar_formula, such as ~ 1 + lnaadt, and rpar_formula can also be a list with a different formula for each outcome.

The likelihood is simulated: each segment’s likelihood is averaged over rpar_draws draws of its random parameters (randomly shifted Halton points), with analytic gradients. The standard deviations are reported as positive; the correlations come from the off-diagonal elements (chol:) of the Cholesky factor.

6. Joint and conditional probabilities

With correlated outcomes, the probability of a combination of counts is not the product of the outcomes’ probabilities. type = "joint" gives the probability of given counts of all outcomes, and type = "conditional" the distribution of one outcome given counts of the others, such as the chance of injury crashes on a segment-year with three PDO crashes:

nd <- w[c(1, 50, 200), ]
predict(mr, newdata = nd, type = "joint", y = c(pdo = 1, injury = 1))
#> [1] 0.008986714 0.001443140 0.012633591
round(predict(mr, newdata = nd, type = "prob", max_count = 2)$injury, 4)
#>           0      1      2
#> [1,] 0.9702 0.0291 0.0006
#> [2,] 0.9868 0.0130 0.0001
#> [3,] 0.9577 0.0409 0.0013
round(predict(mr, newdata = nd, type = "conditional", outcome = "injury",
              given = c(pdo = 3), max_count = 2), 4)
#>           0      1      2
#> [1,] 0.9566 0.0421 0.0013
#> [2,] 0.9738 0.0257 0.0005
#> [3,] 0.9388 0.0585 0.0025
round(predict(mr, newdata = nd, type = "conditional", outcome = "injury",
              given = c(pdo = 0), max_count = 2), 4)
#>           0      1     2
#> [1,] 0.9748 0.0247 4e-04
#> [2,] 0.9872 0.0127 1e-04
#> [3,] 0.9643 0.0348 9e-04

Three PDO crashes raise the probability of an injury crash above its marginal value, and no PDO crash lowers it. Without random parameters (or a copula, Section 7) the equations are independent, and the conditional probabilities equal the marginal ones.

For segments in the estimation data, random = "posterior" uses their own crash history (Empirical Bayes):

seg <- w[w$ID == w$ID[1], ]
round(cbind(predict(mr, newdata = seg),
            predict(mr, newdata = seg, random = "posterior")), 3)
#>        pdo injury   pdo injury
#> [1,] 0.791  0.030 0.539  0.024
#> [2,] 0.786  0.030 0.536  0.024
#> [3,] 0.831  0.031 0.566  0.025

7. Copulas

A copula joins the outcomes’ own distributions into one joint distribution, so each outcome keeps its family and its expected counts:

\[ P(Y_1 \le y_1, Y_2 \le y_2) = C\big(F_1(y_1), F_2(y_2); \theta\big). \]

For counts, the probability of the observed pair is the exact volume of the copula over the rectangle \([F_1(y_1 - 1), F_1(y_1)] \times [F_2(y_2 - 1), F_2(y_2)]\), the sum of four copula values (of \(2^J\) with \(J\) outcomes). For a count far in the upper tail of its distribution both ends of its interval are within rounding of 1, so there the volume is integrated over the upper tail probabilities \(P(Y > y)\) instead, and improbable pairs keep their precision. The copula families differ in the shape of the dependence:

copula dependence
"gaussian" symmetric, any sign, no tail dependence (two outcomes)
"frank" symmetric, no tail dependence; any sign for two outcomes
"clayton" positive, strongest for joint low counts
"gumbel" positive, strongest for joint high counts 
mc <- countreg_mv(cbind(pdo, injury) ~ lnaadt + speed50 + offset(lnlength),
                  data = w, family = c(pdo = "NB2", injury = "PIG"),
                  copula = "clayton")
summary(mc)

summary() reports the copula parameter and its Kendall’s tau. With counts, this tau describes the copula, not the rank correlation of the counts themselves, which is weaker when the counts are small.

Here the copulas barely change the fit, while the MVPLN of section 5 fits much better:

fams <- c(pdo = "NB2", injury = "PIG")
f <- cbind(pdo, injury) ~ lnaadt + speed50 + offset(lnlength)
cop <- lapply(c("gaussian", "frank", "clayton", "gumbel"), function(cf)
  countreg_mv(f, data = w, family = fams, copula = cf))
round(c(independent = AIC(m), gaussian = AIC(cop[[1]]),
        frank = AIC(cop[[2]]), clayton = AIC(cop[[3]]),
        gumbel = AIC(cop[[4]]), MVPLN = AIC(mr)))
#> independent    gaussian       frank     clayton      gumbel       MVPLN 
#>        2510        2511        2511        2511        2512        2472

The two models describe different dependence. The copula links the two counts of the same segment-year; the random intercepts of the MVPLN are shared by a segment’s three years, so it also captures that some segments have more crashes of both kinds year after year. In these data the dependence is mostly of the second kind. A copula suits outcomes measured once per unit, or dependence within a period; random parameters suit repeated observations of the same units.

The two can also be combined: random intercepts shared by a segment’s years, and a copula for what remains within each year.

mb <- countreg_mv(cbind(pdo, injury) ~ lnaadt + speed50 + offset(lnlength),
                  data = w, family = "POISSON", rpar_formula = ~ 1,
                  group_var = "ID", rpar_draws = 200, seed = 1,
                  copula = "frank")

This fit takes about two minutes. Its copula parameter is close to zero (Frank theta 0.06, standard error 0.71), and its AIC (2474.7) is higher than that of the MVPLN alone (2472.1): once the segments’ random intercepts are in the model, nothing is left for the copula. On simulated data with both kinds of dependence, the combined model recovers both.

All parameters of a copula model are estimated together by maximum likelihood (started from the separate equations and the best copula parameter for them), with analytic gradients from the derivatives of the copula and of each outcome’s distribution function. predict() gives joint and conditional probabilities from the copula, and simulate() draws the outcomes from it.
